Saltgrass Steak House · A Texas Energy Pilot · August 2026

Your best nights are your most expensive nights.

A packed dining room loads every system in the building at once. Most of that cost is controllable. This page shows where it hides and how we remove it.

You cannot control the price of cattle. You can control the price of comfort.

Cut food quality and the guest tastes it. Cut labor and the guest feels it. Energy is one of the only line items in a steakhouse that can fall sharply without a single guest noticing, because the fix makes the dining room more comfortable, not less.

A steakhouse is one of the hardest buildings in America to power.

5 to 7× more energy per square foot than a typical commercial building

Cooking gets the blame, but waste is in cooling and refrigeration, running against each other all night. A single electric fryer uses more electricity in a year than the average American home.

On the Gulf Coast, the fight is worse. Every guest at every table adds heat and moisture to the room. The kitchen pumps steam. An air conditioning system older than five to seven years has quietly lost the ability to remove that moisture out of the air. Walk-ins work harder, the dining room feels warmer than the thermostat says, and equipment wears itself out years earlier than needed.

Restaurant industry data puts median full service income before taxes at 2.8 percent of sales. At that margin, every dollar of energy waste we remove does the profit work of roughly 35 dollars of food sales.

Remove a thousand dollars of monthly waste from one restaurant, and it can earn what seating about seven more tables every night earns, without adding a single cook, server, or pound of beef.

The real problem

Every wasted kilowatt bills you three times.

Once on the meter. Once on the demand charge. And once in the life of the equipment. A supply contract, however well negotiated, only ever touches the first one. That is why rate shopping alone has never fixed this bill, and never will.

1

The meter

Cooling and refrigeration are the largest controllable block of a full service restaurant's electric bill. When those systems run inefficiently, every hour of a busy Friday costs more than it should.

2

The demand charge

Delivery charges are set by regulators, not by your supplier, and they are climbing: ERCOT's own forecasts show Texas peak demand growing from about 85 gigawatts toward 139 by 2030 as data centers pour in, and that grid buildout lands on every delivery bill. No broker can negotiate it away.

Needing less power at the peak is the only defense.

3

Equipment life

A unit that runs hot, wet, and hard dies years early. And the 2025 refrigerant transition pushed manufacturer prices on new equipment up roughly 10 percent on top of years of inflation, so every early replacement costs more than the last one did.

Every year of life we recover defers that spend.

The three costs compound in that order: inefficiency raises the peaks, the peaks and the humidity grind down the equipment, and the equipment bill lands in capital budgets years before it should.

The fix, the proof, and the pilot

One lever cuts all three.

Reduce and recondition the cooling and refrigeration load, and the meter drops, the peaks flatten, and the equipment lives longer. We are not a device vendor with one product for every building.
